Title: First-Round Manuscript with English / 中文 Comments
Description: Read a continuous manuscript with paragraph summaries, brief section and paper recaps, linked role-colored highlights, and an English / 中文 comment toggle.

Create an HTML reading companion for my first editorial pass through the supplied manuscript, with switchable English / 中文 commentary. It should feel like reading an article with comments in the margin of a PDF.

I know the general project but may not know its experimental implementation. Help me understand the reported results and the reasoning connecting them to the paper's claims, so I can form my own judgment first.

Project background: [Optional brief context.]

COMMENT LANGUAGE
- Add an English / 中文 toggle to the HTML toolbar, defaulting to English. It changes generated commentary only: margin notes, section/subsection recaps, the final paper recap, local logic checks, the reading map, and the source-coverage note. Keep the original article, quotations, citations, figures, and manuscript headings unchanged.
- The language switch must preserve the analysis, layout, and neutral-first behavior specified here. Write one English set of comments, then produce meaning-equivalent Chinese versions. Do not perform a second independent assessment or introduce new comments, stronger criticism, or different conclusions in Chinese. Preserve references, quantities, conditions, uncertainty, and scope.
- In 中文 mode, use natural Chinese sentence structure and connective language, retaining established academic and field-specific terms in English where natural: for example, claim, baseline, dataset, metric, ablation, generalization, and method names. Aim for the language Chinese-speaking researchers use when discussing work in a US academic setting. Avoid formal translationese, forced code-switching, and parenthetical translations of every term. Ordinary wording can remain Chinese.
- Include both versions in the single HTML file. Use small embedded JavaScript to switch them in place without a network request or runtime translation. Show only the selected language; retain the same comment units, note IDs, source highlights, passage links, article reading position, and open/closed panels. Let longer notes reflow without overlapping the article or other notes. Keep control labels stable and provide Chinese system-font fallbacks for comments.

Example of the desired register, with matching meaning:
English: "The reported output is accuracy on held-out data. The paragraph uses this result to support C2; that connection depends on whether the evaluation set represents the target population."
中文: "这里报告的是 held-out data 上的 accuracy。作者用这个结果来支持 C2；这个联系取决于 evaluation set 是否能代表 target population。"

ARTICLE FIRST
- Preserve the original article in order: title, authors, abstract, headings, paragraphs, figures, captions, tables, equations, citations, and references, wherever supplied.
- Keep paragraphs flowing naturally within each section or subsection. Use ordinary academic typography and spacing. Do not turn paragraphs into cards, numbered blocks, or separate text/comment rows.
- Layout divisions and clearly labeled recap boxes are allowed at section or subsection boundaries. Do not insert explanations, labels, or summaries between paragraphs within a section or subsection.
- Keep the author's wording and qualifications intact. Correct extraction artifacts such as broken line wraps only when the intended text is unambiguous. Identify missing or unreadable material instead of inventing it.

MARGIN COMMENTS
Give every substantive paragraph a concise argument summary in the margin, including paragraphs in the abstract. A single comment may cover consecutive paragraphs when they develop one coherent subargument; identify the full passage range and do not group across section or subsection boundaries. Separate distinct subarguments rather than replacing them with one broad section summary.

Each summary should explain, usually in two or three short sentences or compact labeled lines:
- Claim: what this paragraph or subargument specifically argues or establishes.
- Basis: which result, comparison, observation, citation, or premise it uses, and how that basis connects to the claim. Link to evidence elsewhere when applicable; distinguish reported results from the author's interpretation and any connection you reconstruct.
- Link: how it develops, qualifies, or connects to the larger argument, when this is relevant.

For context, setup, or transitions, summarize what the passage establishes or connects instead of inventing a claim or demanding evidence. A brief one-sentence note may suffice. If a claim has no evidence stated in that passage, describe where the manuscript directs the reader, or state that the basis is not specified; do not supply one yourself.

At relevant experiments, explain the input, high-level operation or comparison, measured output, reported result, and author's interpretation. Keep the measured result distinct from the conclusion drawn from it. Include implementation details only when they change what the result means or what can be inferred, and explain that consequence briefly.

Describe actual claims and evidence rather than merely labeling passages "background" or "provides evidence." Keep the summaries visible by default. Maintain complete coverage even for straightforward passages, using shorter notes rather than omitting them. Figures, tables, and equations should be linked from the summaries that use them.

RECAPS FOR A READER WHO HAS ALREADY READ THE TEXT
- At the end of every substantive section and subsection, add a visible "Section recap" or "Subsection recap." Use one short sentence or a single compact arrow chain, normally no more than 30 English words or equivalently concise Chinese. These supplement the paragraph comments rather than replace them.
- Recall the local argument: what was established, which evidence or premise it used, and how that advances the paper's reasoning. Use the terms, claim IDs, and figure references the reader has just encountered. Preserve essential qualifications, but omit background, definitions, implementation details, and introductory explanation.
- For a parent section with subsections, make its recap connect their contributions rather than repeat each subsection recap. For context or setup sections, recall the premise or question established without inventing an experimental finding.
- Add a final "Paper recap" at the very end of the output, after the reproduced manuscript and brief source-coverage metadata. Limit it to one or two short sentences or a compact reasoning chain, normally no more than 50 English words or equivalently concise Chinese. Recall only the central claim, decisive evidence, and the author's resulting inference, with any indispensable scope restriction.
- Write these as memory cues for someone who has already read the section or paper. They need not stand alone for a new reader. Do not restate the abstract, concatenate all paragraph summaries, introduce new analysis, or give an overall quality verdict.
- Put recaps outside the original prose in compact, clearly labeled boxes at the corresponding end boundaries. Distinguish them from paragraph comments with a neutral background, such as #EDF0F3 for section/subsection recaps and #E2E7EE for the final paper recap. These panel backgrounds indicate scope, not argumentative role or quality. Keep recaps visible by default and include them in the comment-language toggle.

SOURCE HIGHLIGHTS
Keep persistent, clearly visible highlights in the original article, directly matching the summaries in the margin. Highlight meaningful clauses and essential qualifiers, not just isolated keywords. Each substantive summary must link to the exact highlighted passages it describes; when claim and evidence occur in different places, link to both. Use figure, table, or equation anchors for non-prose evidence rather than inventing a textual substitute.

Use the following role colors consistently, with a compact legend:
- Yellow: central claims and subclaims (for example, #FFE066).
- Red: key concepts or definitions (#FFB3B3).
- Blue: reported results, data, or other evidence (#ADD8FF).
- Green: concessions, qualifications, scope limits, or responses to objections (#B7E4B8).
- Purple: experimental setup, methods, or analytical operations (#D6BCFA).

Use distinct background colors with readable dark text, sufficiently saturated to stand out on the paper background. Highlight the spans needed to follow the reasoning, preserving the surrounding continuous text; avoid indiscriminately coloring whole paragraphs. Do not force every color into every passage. Colors indicate argumentative roles, never correctness or issue severity; red concepts are not warnings.

Provide links in both directions between notes and their source highlights. Highlights remain visible without hover or an active note and stay identical when comment language changes. Include text labels for roles so color is not the only cue.

NEUTRAL FIRST PASS
- Do not give an overall verdict, score, praise, recommendation, or strengths-and-weaknesses summary. Do not rewrite the manuscript.
- Distinguish the author's explicit statements, your reconstruction of their connections, and information not specified. Label inferred premises rather than treating them as established facts.
- Preserve the distinctions between reported results and explanations, tested conditions and broader claims, and association and causation.
- Put specific logic concerns in closed-by-default "Local logic check" panels within the relevant margin note. Keep the visible note descriptive; do not preview the concern through warning colors, severity labels, or verdicts.
- Include a check only for a concrete mismatch, ambiguity, contradiction, or missing inferential step. Reference the exact claim and relevant result, explain the connection in question, and state what information would clarify it. Use conditional language when an unstated detail matters.
- Check the relevant supplied sections and appendices before reporting a connection as absent. Missing description does not establish that a procedure was omitted or a claim is false. Avoid speculative problem lists and leading questions.

Example of a local check:
"The reported comparison concerns dataset A under condition B. This sentence extends the claim to condition C; a justification for that extension is not identified in the supplied manuscript."

OPTIONAL READING MAP
Provide a compact, closed-by-default "Reading map" outside the article body, containing:
- The author's stated question, objective, and central claims, with consistent claim IDs.
- One concise entry per experiment: input, operation/comparison, output, reported result, and the claim it is used to address. Explain the stated or inferred reasoning connecting result to claim.
- A two-way index linking central claims to the passages and results that state, develop, or qualify them, and linking relevant margin notes back to those claims. Distinguish mentioning a claim from providing evidence for it.
- A compact coverage index identifying the paragraph or paragraph range assigned to each margin summary, without displaying repeated paragraph labels in the article.

Keep this map descriptive. Label reconstructed links and unstated premises, allow "connection not explicit," and do not assign "supported/unsupported" grades. For non-experimental arguments, map the corresponding premises and conclusions. Do not invent a central claim where the manuscript is ambiguous.

HTML LAYOUT
Produce a single HTML file with embedded CSS and no required external libraries.
- Make the article the main reading surface, with a narrower annotation rail on the right. Group layout by section or subsection, keeping the article text in normal continuous flow within each group.
- Link comments to their passages using small, neutral markers and working links in both directions. Keep anchor IDs unobtrusive; do not display paragraph numbers or repeated metadata throughout the article.
- Place notes near their anchors where practical. Stack notes with compact spacing; where necessary, let them continue at the end of the corresponding section or subsection. Do not omit or hide the base summaries to reduce crowding, or add gaps between article paragraphs to force exact alignment. Notes must not overlap or cover the text.
- Include compact sticky section navigation and a show/hide margin-notes control. Keep the reading map and local logic checks closed initially so the article dominates the first view.
- On narrow screens, collect the visible base summaries after the corresponding section or subsection, with links back to the highlighted passages, followed by that unit's recap. Keep only local logic checks collapsed; preserve uninterrupted paragraph flow within the article.
- Use an off-white paper background, restrained navy controls, readable serif article text, and smaller sans-serif notes. Lora and IBM Plex Sans are optional, with system-font fallbacks. Preserve the visible role-colored highlights specified above and the continuous article layout.

ACCURACY AND COVERAGE
Use precise source references without inventing quotations, values, page numbers, or findings from cited work. Label supplementary background. Report unavailable figures or other source material explicitly.

Place a brief coverage note before the final Paper recap, identifying any omitted or unreadable source material and, separately, any paragraphs or subarguments left without summaries. Do not silently skip content or imply complete coverage when the source or output is incomplete. The Paper recap is the final block; do not append an overall assessment or editing-priority list.
