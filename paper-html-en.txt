Title: Understand a Paper’s Argument
Description: Build understanding through an argument map, close reading of key passages, explanations of decisive evidence, and self-check questions.

Create an English-language reading guide for the supplied academic paper.

The goal is to help me explain, in my own words:
- What question the paper addresses.
- What it contributes.
- Why its evidence or reasoning supports its claims.
- What assumptions and limitations affect its conclusions.

READER CONTEXT
My background: [Specify; otherwise assume a university-educated reader unfamiliar with this field.]
Reading purpose: [General understanding / seminar discussion / evaluating the research / applying it.]
Close-reading scope: [Default: selected key passages. Optional: every paragraph.]

CONTENT

1. Start with the big picture
Explain:
- What specific question motivates the paper, and why it matters.
- What previous explanations, methods, or knowledge leave unresolved.
- The central contribution in one precise sentence, preserving important qualifications.
- What would change in our understanding if the author is right.

Avoid vague statements such as “offers valuable insights.” Explain what the insight actually is.

2. Map the argument
Identify the main claims and how they connect. For each central claim, explain:
- What exactly is being claimed.
- What evidence, derivation, or textual analysis supports it.
- Why that support counts as a reason to accept the claim.
- What assumptions connect the support to the conclusion.
- How far the conclusion extends and what remains unresolved.

Adapt the structure to the paper. Empirical, theoretical, methodological, and interpretive papers may require different forms of analysis.

Provide verifiable references to sections, pages, figures, tables, or equations. Do not invent page numbers.

3. Explain the decisive material
Select the figures, results, equations, or passages that matter most to understanding the paper.

For each, explain:
- How to read it.
- Which details matter.
- How it advances the argument.

Explain essential concepts in accessible language, followed by their precise meaning. Use concrete examples when helpful, clearly distinguishing examples from the paper from your own teaching examples.

4. Annotate key passages
By default, select the passages most important to the argument and briefly explain why you chose them.

Left column:
- The original passage, preserving wording, numbers, and qualifications.
- A reference identifying its location.

Right column:
- The passage’s function and connection to the surrounding argument.
- The explanation most useful for understanding it.
- Any specific argumentative technique, unstated assumption, or limitation worth examining.

Annotations must discuss the passage’s actual content. Labels such as “provides evidence” or “introduces the topic” are insufficient on their own.

Identify weaknesses only when there is a concrete basis for doing so. Not every passage needs criticism or every annotation category.

5. Check understanding
Finish with:
- The three most important takeaways.
- Three self-check questions: one about the central idea, one about the relationship between evidence and claim, and one about the conclusion’s boundaries.
- Suggested answers in separate collapsible sections so I can try answering first.

ACCURACY
- Distinguish the author’s explicit statements, your reconstruction of the argument, and supplementary background.
- Preserve uncertainty, conditions, and the distinction between association and causation where relevant.
- Do not fabricate quotations, data, citations, or counterarguments.
- Identify missing, unreadable, or uncertain material.
- Support evaluations with specific details from the paper.

HTML OUTPUT
Produce a single HTML file with embedded CSS.

Place the overview and argument map first, followed by the annotated passages and self-check questions.

Include:
- Sticky section navigation.
- A two-column desktop layout: original passages on the left, corresponding commentary on the right.
- A mobile layout that places each passage immediately before its commentary.
- A deep navy navigation bar and off-white page background.
- Lora for original passages and IBM Plex Sans for interface text, with system-font fallbacks.
- Commentary cards with a subtle background and colored left border.

Highlight only essential phrases:
- Yellow: central claims.
- Red: key concepts.
- Blue: evidence.
- Green: concessions or responses to objections.
- Purple: methods.

Include a legend and text labels so meaning does not depend on color alone.
